In 1994, the American violinist Lisa Germano meticulously crafted the masterpiece rock album ‘Geek the Girl’ from a humble studio in her house. Using a very sparse and apparently straightforward arrangement of sounds, she paints a dark, devastating world with musical finesse that very few before her had managed to achieve. Her delicate whispers are accompanied by a harrowing atmosphere produced by the violin, guitar and drums. Her soft voice almost conceals the lyrical genius and depth of her songs. Through these lyrics she portrays herself as a broken and abused young woman being bullied in a cruel world. Furthermore, almost throughout the entirety of the album, she only talks about ‘you and I’ making the album a personal collage of her vulnerability.  

It is mind-boggling how the entire world is sharing an open secret so carefully—the secret of our fragility. Up until very recently, trauma had always been banished to some corner of the brain. Nobody dealt with it; they just ran away from it—and running away from it never worked. It always stayed in the same corner where you threw it, ready to explode like a bomb if disturbed from its slumber. Except this bomb could detonate multiple times, even an infinite number of times if not confronted. So everybody basically distracted themselves, threw up a façade, swallowed down their fears, and warily walked on through their lives; but they always had a uneasy feeling that something bad will happen. That trauma didn’t disappear; like a wounded wolf, it attacks whenever it feels threatened. And like a wounded wolf, it can cause a lot of damage—especially if that wolf has power and could make big decisions.  

Trauma develops when the brain is exposed to shocking stimuli or events. That imprint stays in the brain, and continuous to flare up periodically—especially in environments similar to the environment in which it developed— if it is not disarmed. Modern psychiatric techniques such as exposure therapy have proven to be competent at doing just that. Basically, the brain is slowly made to unlearn that imprint, and that is the only way of overcoming trauma. Even if one doesn’t go for clinical procedures to deal with it, this can be done by simply talking about it in ‘safe spaces’ and exposing yourself to the trigger safely, and the brain slowly starts to stop panicking whenever it senses the trigger.  

In man’s flight from trauma, Religion has always been his best friend. God has somewhat become everybody’s personal therapist—but quite an incompetent one. When traumatised people couldn’t confide in their fears to others (remember, they were busy putting up a façade), their only option was God; and God told them that he will take care of it… and that you should carry on with life. It also helped that God told people what to do with their lives— which includes generic life advice (raise a family, be nice to others), and more importantly that everything important that there was to know is already known, yet at the same time being so vague about it that it can be interpreted in any way. All hallmarks of bad epistemology, but I digress. To help man distract himself from his fear and trauma, there were always many addictions to choose from. More on this later.  

As I have written in a previous essay, God served one very important purpose which was needed for traumatised people: to give closure. They just wanted to feel safe, and God gave them that. And whenever this closure was threatened when evidence suggested otherwise, the vagueness of Religion allowed them to always wiggle out of it. But the fact of the matter is that God is just wishful thinking. It will just never work. God was never the solution to the problem, only a distraction. Even if some religious person overcame trauma, it was due to some other factors, and God could always be filtered out of the equation. As my physics teacher once used to say, “If you stare at a problem, it will stare back at you.” Or as the YouTuber Ryan Higa once sang—

Do not expect progress if you don’t seek it. But is progress ever possible?  

A thousand years ago, and perhaps maybe up until the late 17th century (and hell some even today) people would have answered you no. Things never changed too much over the years, and even if they did, they did so very slowly; it was very easy to forget that things could get better at all. This completely changed with The Enlightenment. Progress emerged rapidly, and the rate of progress kept on increasing. But why?  

The book ‘Beginning of Infinity’ by David Deutsch answers this beautifully. Progress came from people seeking hard-to-vary explanations (complimented by thorough criticism and testing). For Newton, saying the apple falls when released was not enough. He dared to make the claim that it falls precisely as a quadratic expression of time, with a very specific acceleration. When Galileo released the pendulum, he wasn’t sated with saying that the bob went back and forth. No, he dared to claim that its time period stayed constant throughout its motion which itself depended on a very precise relationship between its length and the local gravitational acceleration. These are non-trivial statements about physical reality. It is sort of hard to believe that nature follows these laws that we cooked up with utmost discipline (in modern day physics, quantum electrodynamics makes predictions which accurate to an absurd number of significant digits—sometimes even more than 10), and it isn’t just ‘random’.  

The physicist Eugene Wigner referred to this as ‘The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in Natural Sciences’. Well, it isn’t exactly unreasonable, it is just mind-boggling. It has to do with the explicability of the Universe. But why in the world is the Universe explicable? Due to the reasoning originally given by the physicist Richard Feynman, we would not have existed if the Universe was inexplicable — or ‘random’. The Universe could never have been stable enough, diverse enough, or even formed complex enough structures for life to emerge if it were inexplicable. And as the Physicist David Deutsch argues in his book ‘The Beginning of Infinity’, our Universe must be explicable at all levels of emergence. We simply cannot have a logically consistent framework in which there is explicability in some level of emergence (which we know exists because of Science) but inexplicability in levels beyond or beneath it.   

This peculiar behaviour of human beings of accepting inexplicability is very interesting. This has always happened in the past at various levels of civilisation and at various levels of emergence in our knowledge. The physicist Philipp von Jolly, under whom Max Planck was a student, in 1878 famously advised him to not go into theoretical physics saying "[it is a] highly developed, nearly fully matured science, that through the crowning achievement of the discovery of the principle of conservation of energy will arguably soon take its final stable form." It was quite outrageous for Jolly to make such a statement then, when there were many massive problems in physics such as why is the Earth so old, or what powers the Sun or what charges were or some anomalies in thermodynamics (Later we had even more massive problems in the foundations of physics such as the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment, the Ultraviolet Catastrophe, the Photo-electric effect and so on, the stability of the atom). We knew that something was very wrong with the foundations itself.   

Therefore, when Jolly made his statement in 1878, he was either expecting all these critical problems in the theory to be solved within the classical framework, or he expecting these phenomena to not have an explanation at all. In the former case, how can one even make such a claim? How can you know how a problem is going to be solved when you do not know its solution? In the latter case, my answer is the same as above — you cannot have a logically consistent framework with explicability at one level of emergence and inexplicability at another. You cannot have regularity from irregularity.   

I think in both the cases, the reason one makes such a claim is that it is hard for people to believe that there is a whole new world out there, a world that we know very little about. The theories that we have at any level of emergence (in the example of Jolly it is the level of classical mechanics and classical fields) work marvellously well for an incredibly large number of cases. For example, in most of Astrophysics and Astronomy, even to this day we use Newtonian gravitation and mechanics. When any field in Natural Sciences (not just Physics) is at a point where a given hard-to-vary framework (for example Mendelian genetics) at a given level of emergence has been formulated, it is sort of hard to believe and accept that something that works so well in a myriad number of cases could ultimately be wrong.   

This feature of frameworks at a given level of emergence to succeed at almost everything except a few cases has proven to be very competent at deceiving people into thinking that we have a final theory in any given field. This has happened many times in history. Before The Enlightenment, people were very confident that they knew all there was to know—be it Natural Philosophy, Medicine, Moral Philosophy, Agriculture and so on. Funnily enough, we are at another point in our history where an appallingly large number of people—even Academics—are convinced that Physics is drawing towards an end! The fact of the matter is that until we are at a point where we have no more questions in the foundations of physics (or any natural science), we cannot expect that we have achieved the final theory; leaving some questions to nonexplanation just won’t cut it. And I really doubt if we shall ever reach such a point where we just do not have any questions in the foundations, and certainly not today when we actually have the most number of unsolved problems in the foundations.  

You will have to forgive me for taking the majority of examples from Physics to make my point about the nature of frameworks in any Natural Science field: I am simply more familiar with it than other fields—that’s all (please don’t accuse me of being racist, I have a fragile heart). My conclusions should hold for any field of science; I shall go into more detail about this in a future essay. You will also have to forgive me for sneaking a large discussion on epistemology into an essay in moral philosophy, but the two fields are much more intertwined than most imagine. This is one of the main focuses of this essay.   

Speaking of epistemology having a big effect on moral philosophy, the era of particle physics saw the emergence of the ‘existential physics’ movement. The age-old argument that the laws of physics are deterministic and hence one does not have any free will was revived—this time with the addition of probabilistic phenomena as a result of quantum physics. The problem with this argument is—and it has always has been so (by this I mean that even if somehow magically tomorrow we discover that nature doesn’t have any probabilistic component at all and the quantum framework was a deception)— it is excessively reductionist. As Deutsch argues, the properties of a system composed of smaller objects which interact in a specific way is very different from these composite objects. This phenomenon is known as emergence. One of the most popular examples is classical thermodynamics, and another is life.   

It is clear that we understand very, very little about the brain and consciousness. This is one of the biggest, most challenging, interesting and exciting problems in biology. Again, there are people who argue that we never will understand consciousness: but that is just nonsense. We know all the underlying components of the brain—molecules and so on and how they interact with each other. We just need to figure out how these interactions give rise to the emergent property we call consciousness (it is only a matter of computation and approximation—but a hard one). But this level of emergence does exist. At the emergent level of thoughts, things like emotions, decision making are all very real phenomena. They are not ‘illusions’, in the same way that Newton’s gravity isn’t an illusion. It is clear that the underlying interactions in our brain roughly approximate to what we call as free will.  There is no need to hurry and claim that it is an illusion. Usually, the only people interesting in making such claims are the ones that don’t want to justify their actions—and hence can act in any way they wish. So for example, I can stab you and claim that I had no free will in doing anything about it. This is quite a dramatic example, but one can see that denying free will leads to nonsensical situations: that is, something called free will does exist to some degree.   

Over the years, I have seen many people make big decisions without any good explanations for them. Naturally, I am curious about people’s behaviour and why they make certain decisions: so I ask them why they want to do something over the other. Sometimes I do get good answers, and I am really glad to hear them—but by and large, I do not get any good explanations. When further prompted to give a good reason, the large majority of the times the questioning ends with “I don’t know, it is just the way I am.” Surely there is an objective answer to the question of whether or not I should stab you (I can’t stab you just because “I like it, I don’t know why” or “it feels right”  or “I feel like it” or “I was born to do it” or “it came to me in a dream” or “god wants me to do it”, I need a very good reason), so why isn’t there one for all other moral questions? Of course, as in any field, there are easy problems and hard problems: some decisions need to be thought a lot about before one comes to a sensible conclusion, and for some, you run out of time before you figure out a good plan. Currently though, a large number of people give up trying to come up with reasons, and a lot of the times this is accompanied by the wishful thinking that is God as if it wasn’t bad enough originally. This is the philosophy of the ‘go with the flow’ crowd in a nutshell. I think it is appropriate that this must be paraphrased as the ‘act without thinking rationally’ crowd (I can’t exactly go with the flow and kill you, can I?). Look—sometimes you don’t have enough time to think deeply about some decision, you just need to pull the trigger. That is completely fine; all I am saying is that do not expect mistakes to not creep up in those cases. But when one makes none of their decisions based on good explanations—which surely exist, we just need to find them— they really shouldn’t expect anything of their lives, unless they get astronomically lucky (even luck has explanation). The last people who acted without proper reason were the alchemists and the astrologers, and look how they ended up! As you can see, a lot of bad moral behaviour is the result of bad epistemology, and this was an example of the deep connection between it and moral philosophy that I was referring to earlier.  

Bad epistemology has been running rampant causing many more problems in moral philosophy. The naïve reductionist approach to consciousness has caused some more trouble too. A popular argument is, “If I am a sack of atoms, what is the point of life? Aren’t all my actions, desires and dreams meaningless?” Stephen Hawking once famously remarked, “[humans are] just a chemical scum on the surface of a typical planet that’s in orbit round a typical star on the outskirts of a typical galaxy.”  We are insignificant—and our lives too— in the ‘cosmic scheme of things’. This is commonly referred to as the Principle of Mediocrity. It is a big misconception, as beautifully demonstrated by Deutsch in the chapter ‘The Spark’ of his book which you can look into as I can’t explain it any better.  

The philosophy sounds innocent enough—what harm could it do? But alas, one could not have been more wrong! This has been used to justify all kinds of horrible attitudes towards life. The foremost is Hedonism, which is basically ‘party, party, party’. Living life just to get pleasure out of addictions is pathetic.  All you look forward to in life is the next ‘hit’. This hit can come from a lot of things—some of the most popular ones being drugs, sex, alcohol. Between these hits, life is hell: all these addictions are very taxing on your body as they put you in extreme conditions, and in the period between hits, all this exhaustion hits you at once. These addictions are a lot of the times coupled with poor overall health, which only makes the problem so much worse. This is why drug withdrawal is so painful. You never had anything to live for in the first place, the drugs were only a distraction. What did you expect?   

Actually, the most brutal and vicious addictions are not the ones I mentioned above, but the ones which are sneaky and innocent-looking—and the ones which can easily end your life…without you ever noticing. One of the powerful ones is living for external validation. You just live to get compliments from others. Pretty people, actors are the ones who are the most susceptible—but so are successful people. Every time someone tells them they’re great, they get a hit, and their life is hell when they are not in a hit. A close cousin is ‘living for respect’, or izzat. Living just to preserve the respect of your lineage, your family or yourself can have devastating consequences. Since your life was never more than what others thought about you, when they start to think that you’re not really ‘the dawg’, violence starts looking like a pretty girl you see on the metro whom you will never meet again.  This was beautifully shown in the 2021 Kannada film Garuda Gamana Vrishabha Vahana. Sometimes the consequences are milder, but there is almost always damage dealt in an attempt to gain validation or preserve respect.  

Another potent drug is Altruism. We as humans have a duty to help each other out. It is a horrible feeling seeing others in pain—and life can be very painful— and I am very happy that a very large number of people are empathetic and want to help them. It really is heart-warming to see videos online where strangers rush to help someone who gets into an accident on the road. That instinct to unconditionally help others that humans possess is just fantastic, and when you help someone in pain, you can change the entire course of their lives. But you should also do it sincerely. Let me explain with the help of the following thought experiment:  

The time is 10,000 BCE and a clan called Gaylords lived in the Indus River Valley. Agriculture had newly been invented, and the clan reaped its benefits by settling down and setting up civilization in the serene and comfortable valley. The times were nice, and the population of the clan had tripled over the last 100 hundred years, thanks to the large availability of nutritious food. However, over a span of few years, pests start breeding heavily in the surrounding regions of the valley due to changes in climatic variables. From then on, every year, the wheat crop was decimated by the voracious swarm, and many people started dying. Uptham, a smart and hardworking young man, couldn’t bear to see so many people starving, and especially not the children. He worked really hard in the fields along with his brothers, and planted a lot of seeds of the crop in the hope that he will be left with more of the crop after the anticipated pests’ attack. But it was still not enough to feed the starving population. Uptham, however, kept going; he would work as hard as possible and plant more seeds, hoping that one day he will be able to help those people just by planting a lot more. Deep down in his heart he knows it is impossible: to save the clan, smart ideas are required, not just hard work; but he keeps going, telling himself that he is at least helping others, so how could he be being doing something wrong?  

On the other side of town, Sadayan too is very troubled by the famine. He spends many long hours in the fields and thinks very hard about what to do. Suddenly, he is struck by a flash of inspiration: why not plant Millets instead of Wheat?! He had noticed that the Millet plants he had seen in the surrounding area were left untouched by the swarm. The clan previously had never adopted the crop, as it didn’t taste as great as wheat and they didn’t know how to use it to make healthy edible food. Over the following few weeks, Sadayan plants his first crop of Millets. In the meantime, he tries to figure out how to process and cook it using the grains he extracted from the wild. Inspired by how wheat grains are used, he makes an incredible invention: making rotis with millets! During this time however, he kept being taunted and harassed for not helping to grow wheat like the others. He was considered crazy by many in the village, and not the least of all his own family. They couldn’t understand why he was embarking on the foolhardy mission of trying to domesticate millets, and not picking the realistic goal of growing more wheat like Uptham (who was hailed as a town hero). They told him he could be helping so many people, and that he was committing a sin by letting those people starve.  

Sadayan would not be disheartened. He kept going on. As the time for harvesting the first millet crop came closer, the pests attacked the town and razed the wheat fields; but the millet fields grown by Sadayan stood tall and proud. The entire town was shocked. They finally understood the genius idea of Sadayan, and from then on the town started growing and eating millets, and the starving people were fed. The Gaylords were saved, and they went on to become the most powerful clan in the valley.  

We as humans have a duty to help each other out, but we must also do it sincerely. Blind Altruism can cause more damage than good. Yes, it feels good to donate to a beggar, but what use is it if they use it to buy weed and not make their lives better. You cannot just tell yourself that you’re at least a good person for giving money to a beggar, and not solve the actual problem. Yes, sometimes the problems are very hard to solve like poverty, but it is also true that it is not impossible (another example of epistemology’s impact on moral philosophy). You cannot just help and feel good about it, you must also think whether you are actually making a change or at least think whether you are causing good or harm. To not do so is selfish, it is a drug. You are ‘helping’ people not with the intention of causing a real change, but just to make yourself feel better. In the story above, Uptham knew that his plan was not going to work out, but he just kept going by telling himself that he was at least being ‘helpful’. Uptham was as smart as Sadayan, but he never tried to actually solve the problem because he was too busy being hailed as the nice guy of the village. What if Sadayan had never existed? The problem may have never been solved, and the Gaylords would’ve perished—all because Uptham was too selfish to not solve the actual problem. This is what I meant by Altruism being an addiction.  

There are lots of programmes in Philanthropy where the emphasis is on a completely wrong part of the problem. It is usually on treating the symptoms and not finding the cure. Of course, treating the symptoms is important too, but one should know that without the cure (solution requiring creative effort), the problem will never get solved. And when one works on just the symptoms because they just had a mid-life crisis and realized that they have done nothing their whole life and have recently taken up philanthropy because ‘they are at least doing something’— and never work on the cure, it is a drug; because the reason they’re in it is not to make an actual change, but just to feel better about themselves.   

Both the examples of addiction that I gave you above are a result of bad moral philosophy which ultimately did stem from bad epistemology—from the principle of mediocrity and by extension the misconception that problems are not solvable. I hope I have demonstrated how much damage it can cause.  

But there is much more damage the misconception that progress isn’t possible (which is just problems are insoluble paraphrased) can cause: a phenomenon that I call Intellectual Death. Unfortunately, there comes a time in most people’s life when they just stop trying to do anything new, stop trying to learn anything new, stop trying to criticize their own knowledge. They are convinced that they know everything, and that all progress is only superficial. This sentiment has actually plagued humanity since the beginning of its existence, and only very recently Science seems to have fended it off in its domain (before people use to think that scientific progress was impossible too) —although not completely. There are still people who believe that there will come a time when humans will just be too dumb to solve problems; that is, we will hit an unsurmountable barrier —and today is as good a time as ever to have hit it. Maybe we will never discover the cure to cancer, maybe we will never understand consciousness, maybe we will never figure out how to deflect an asteroid and so on. All this is complete nonsense as I (and Deutsch) have argued.  

Such sentiments also plague moral philosophy: maybe I will never overcome my fears, maybe I am never meant to be happy, maybe the point of life is suffering and so on. This causes Intellectual Death in behaviour. People just stop trying to better themselves, because what is there worth becoming. They justify their bad actions by saying that there was nothing good they could’ve done anyway.  

I guess the most brutal consequence of bad epistemology in moral philosophy is that people think that there is nothing to do in their lives. There is nothing special about humans, nothing out there in there to discover or create, life never gets better, and none of their problems ever get solved. Almost an year ago, I had conjectured that the meaning of life to be “To create beautiful art in any field you may pursue.” The thing about creating something beautiful (be it in music, physics, politics, dance) is it is very difficult to do so. This is because to create something beautiful, that is show something that has never been shown before, you have to think up things which have never been thought before, invent new techniques that never have been used before and so on.   

Even to experience good art, a lot of effort is required from the side of the audience. They must be ready for change, a transformation, to see something about the world that they hadn’t seen before. This process requires a lot of energy, because your brain simply isn’t familiar with what has been created—it simply doesn’t know what it doesn’t know. When one doesn’t expect to learn the Theory of Relativity without effort, how can one expect to understand Kafka’s books without?   

A lot of people don’t put this effort, and hence stop experiencing new art after a given point—if there at all was ever a point when they did. They just keep watching, reading, listening to the old template stuff over and over again. They know that they are doing this, but never do anything else because they don’t know any better. This only furthers the feeling that there is nothing out there to be created/experienced. Everything in life dissolves into a grey blur: there is nothing to see and nothing to do. This is Intellectual Death.   

The only way to ever escape out of this is to try doing new things (especially things that contradict your world view, or sound like moonshine), and always being ready to put in the work. Curiosity is your best friend in this endeavour. When I was younger, I used to think of philosophy as joke: something bearded white men used to squabble about and reach no conclusions (blame my parents for bringing me up this way lol). But one day, I came across an advertisement on Instagram for a book called The Beginning of Infinity. I thought the name sounded cool (c’mon it has infinity in the title!) and began to read it. It was really difficult and confusing, but I kept reading it simply because I was curious. Once I began to understand it, I knew he cooked—and I never looked back.  

Almost a year after that, I had formulated some naïve views on art and presented it to a friend. He told me that there is this random egoistic Italian man online who thought my ideology was garbage. I was confident he was wrong, but again I was curious and did listen to what he thought was good music. It was very difficult, but once I understood it, I knew he cooked—and I never looked back.    

One must always be ready to change, ready to question their deepest knowledge. There are never perfect theories. Yes, they will function brilliantly at a given level of emergence, but ultimately, they will be superseded by something more fundamental down the line. Our best knowledge will have flaws. One must also always be willing to put in hard work, because nothing brilliant ever comes out of a cakewalk. One must also keep in mind that the most beautiful things come from doing the hardest things. The US president John. F. Kennedy once remarked in a speech in 1962 referring to the Apollo missions, “We want to go to the moon—not because it is easy, but because it is hard!”  

Ironically, I have heard many smart people doubt themselves, “What if I am not good enough to solve these hard problems?” This is again a result of bad moral philosophy stemming from bad epistemology. Of course, you may not be as smart as Einstein, but the problems that are to be solved are only hard, not impossible. Do not settle for something less out of fear, you may not have enough time later for the hard problems (which as I said earlier, have the greatest reward). Of course, being smart and not going after impossible-sounding problems at the get-go is fine (if you do something too difficult in the beginning you might get demotivated), but that’s all that they are— impossible-sounding, not impossible.    

I asked a friend once what he wanted from his life—he replied, “To surround myself with friends and loved ones who can comfort me in difficult times.” I have many friends who have gone through tough spots in their life, and some who have who gone through devastating ordeals. I can only imagine the pain they are subjected to, and the trauma they develop as a result. Doubtless, it is a horrible thing to go through, but I ultimately think that humans are much more than their scars. Trauma will fade (of course, the time taken varies with the intensity) with time, only if you find something worth doing in your life. And there are an infinite number of things that are worth doing with your life: humans are magicians who have the possibility to create and do practically anything. I have written about this in previous essays.  

So to live life only to survive sounds like a hell that you’ve subjected yourself to. You will never become anything beyond your fears and trauma unless you find a purpose beyond them. And the only purpose that I know which doesn’t crumble when subjected to rigorous criticism is the one that I have written in Purpose (Essay). You can’t live life in constant fear of what could happen, and filling the empty parts of it with addictions. In the book ‘Man’s Search for Meaning (1946)’, Victor Frankl a holocaust survivor, proposes the meaning of life is subjective and specifically that one can find meaning in suffering as well. But you are so much more than your suffering.   

Society is plagued by bad parenting: parents are too afraid of what could happen if they let their kid go—to let them explore what life is, to do difficult things, to search for answers. The parents don’t believe in progress; they think that their lives —and problems in general— cannot be overcome and the only thing you can ever do is to stay safe. This very well explains the Engineering/Medical fanatism in India. Once you get an IIT or AIIMS seat, your future is ‘secured’, and you can enjoy your salary, raise a family and be safe. Don’t do anything crazy, because you will fail (Deutsch named this the ‘Precautionary Principle’). It is what my father told me. Perhaps the quote by Cooper from the movie Interstellar (2014) will be appropriate here, “We have forgotten who we are Tom—sailers, pioneers—not caretakers.”   

Bad things will keep happening. Terrible things will keep happening. Mistakes will always crop into everything we do. But we just can’t let them define us. We have to correct them and move on. Life is too short you to just wallow and be captivated by fear. Life is too beautiful for you to just wallow and be captivated by fear. Don’t let your life run by you like a fast horse; grab it—and ride it. I cant even imagine what you can achieve.

My chess coach had once kept the following quote as his status on WhatsApp, “Who are you when no one’s watching.” The large majority of the people cannot sit alone by themselves. Some of the times it is because the silence brings back flashes of a traumatic past or the fears associated with them; some of the times it is because they have given up that anything useful could ever come out of their thoughts. In the latter case, they just need to be surrounded by people (and addictions) all the time, because they never were living life to do something beautiful—to create something that transcends you; they were never living life for themselves. It just makes me really sad to see fellow humans who have so much potential, who can do incredible things, can solve incredible problems, can help decrease sorrow by incredible amounts, just waste their life away.   

In the former case in the second sentence of the above paragraph—  

I know that you’re in so much pain  
I know that it feels like there’s no way out  
But darling I know you’re greater than that  
So will you please just take my hand?
